Before loading any data, Cell 1 sets up the basic tools for the notebook:

### Step 1: Inspecting the Raw Data Before Computing (Cells 2 & 3)
Before calculating averages or merging tables, Cells 2 and 3 inspect `.shape`, `.info()`, `.describe()`, and `.isna().sum()` because Pandas silently ignores `NaN` values during calculations.

Running Cells 2 & 3 reveals **5 important data issues** in the raw CSV files:
1. **Different Row Counts (Granularity Mismatch):**
   - `energy_raw` has **`35,064` rows** (1 row per hour across 4 years for all of Spain: $4 \times 365 \times 24 + 24\text{ leap hours} = 35,064$).
   - `weather_raw` has **`178,396` rows** because it records hourly weather for **5 Spanish cities** (`Madrid`, `Barcelona`, `Valencia`, `Seville`, `Bilbao`).
2. **2 Completely Empty Columns in `energy_raw` (`35,064` NaNs):**
   - `generation hydro pumped storage aggregated` and `forecast wind offshore eday ahead` have `0 non-null` values (100% empty).
3. **6 All-Zero Columns in `energy_raw`:**
   - `generation fossil coal-derived gas`, `oil shale`, `peat`, `geothermal`, `marine`, and `wind offshore` are `0.0` across the entire dataset. They carry no useful signal.
4. **Small Sensor Gaps (`17–36` missing hours):**
   - `total load actual` (our main prediction target) is missing `36` hours out of `35,064`.
5. **Impossible Weather Outliers in `weather_raw.describe()` (Cell 3):**
   - **`pressure`** has a minimum of **`0` hPa** and a maximum of **`1,008,371` hPa** (`1.008e+06`)! Normal Earth atmospheric pressure is between `900` and `1,080` hPa. Those `244` corrupted sensor readings caused the standard deviation (`std`) to jump to `5,969`.
   - **`wind_speed`** has a maximum of **`133` m/s** (`478 km/h`), which is faster than a Category 5 hurricane (`3` corrupted sensor rows).

### Step 2: Cleaning `energy_dataset.csv` (Cell 4)
Function `clean_energy(df)` fixes the energy DataFrame in one clean method chain:
1. **`.drop(columns=drop_cols)`**: Removes the 2 completely empty columns and 6 all-zero columns (reducing `29` columns down to `21`).
2. **`.assign(time=lambda d: pd.to_datetime(d['time'], utc=True))`**: Converts the text timestamp string into a UTC `datetime64` column so Python handles chronological sorting and daylight saving time properly.
3. **`.sort_values('time').reset_index(drop=True)`**: Orders the rows strictly from oldest (`2014-12-31 23:00 UTC`) to newest (`2018-12-31 22:00 UTC`).
4. **`.ffill().bfill()`**: Fills the tiny 17–36 hour sensor gaps using **forward-fill** (carrying the previous hour's value forward). Why not fill with the 4-year column mean? Because using a 4-year mean uses *future* values to fill past gaps (data leakage).
5. **`.dt.hour`, `.dt.month`, `.dt.dayofweek`**: Extracts calendar features (hour `0–23`, month `1–12`, day of week `0=Mon` to `6=Sun`) so our plots and models know the time of day and day of week.
6. **Three `assert` checks**: Automatically verify that no rows were lost (`len(out) == 35,064`), every timestamp is unique (`out['time'].is_unique`), and zero `NaN`s remain.

### Step 3: Cleaning `weather_features.csv`, Grouping & Safe Merge (Cell 5)
Function `clean_and_aggregate_weather(df)` prepares the weather data and merges it onto `energy`:
1. **Fix `' Barcelona'` leading space (`d['city_name'].str.strip().astype('category')`)**:
   - In the raw CSV, Barcelona was recorded as `" Barcelona"` with a leading space. `.str.strip()` removes the space, and `.astype('category')` stores city names efficiently in memory.
2. **Remove `3,076` Duplicate City-Hour Rows (`.drop_duplicates(subset=['time', 'city_name'], keep='first')`)**:
   - $35,064\text{ hours} \times 5\text{ cities} = 175,320$ rows. The raw file had `178,396` rows because `3,076` city-hour timestamps were logged twice with different text descriptions (such as `"rain"` and `"thunderstorm"`). Dropping duplicates on `['time', 'city_name']` leaves exactly `175,320` rows (`assert len(w) == 35064 * 5`).
3. **Mask Sensor Outliers with `.loc[mask, col]`**:
   - We flag impossible sensor readings: `bad_pressure = (w['pressure'] < 900) | (w['pressure'] > 1080)` (`244` rows) and `bad_wind = w['wind_speed'] > 50` (`3` rows).
   - We replace them with `np.nan` using `w.loc[bad_pressure, 'pressure'] = np.nan` and forward-fill within each city (`w.groupby('city_name', observed=True)[['pressure', 'wind_speed']].ffill().bfill()`).
4. **Difference Between `transform` and `agg`**:
   - **`.groupby('city_name', observed=True)['temp'].transform('mean')`**: Computes each city's average temperature while **keeping all `175,320` rows**, allowing us to subtract it from `w['temp']` to get `temp_dev_from_city_mean` (how warm or cold that hour is compared to that city's normal climate).
   - **`.groupby('time', as_index=False).agg(...)`**: Collapses the **5 cities at each hour** into **1 national summary row per hour** (`35,064` rows), calculating `temp_mean`, `temp_min`, `temp_max`, `temp_dev_mean`, `pressure_mean`, `humidity_mean`, `wind_speed_mean`, and `clouds_all_mean`.
5. **Safe 1-to-1 Merge with `assert` Checks**:
   - `assert weather_hourly['time'].is_unique`: Confirms the aggregated weather table has exactly 1 row per timestamp before merging.
   - `merged = energy.merge(weather_hourly, on='time', how='left', validate='one_to_one')`: Joins weather onto energy.
   - `assert len(merged) == before`: Confirms the merge kept all `35,064` rows (`32` columns total) with `0` unmatched rows.

### Step 5: Chronological Split, Parquet Saving & `float32` Standardization (Cell 7)
1. **Why Split by Time Instead of Random `train_test_split`?**
   - If you shuffle time-series data randomly, `14:00` on Tuesday might be in `Train` while `13:00` on the same Tuesday is in `Test`. The model could cheat by memorizing adjacent hours (temporal data leakage).
   - Instead, we slice strictly in chronological order using `.iloc`:
     - **Train (first 70%):** `24,544` hours (`Dec 31, 2014` → `Oct 19, 2017`)
     - **Validation (next 15%):** `5,260` hours (`Oct 19, 2017` → `May 26, 2018`)
     - **Test (final 15%):** `5,260` hours (`May 26, 2018` → `Dec 31, 2018`)
   - Our `assert train_df['time'].max() < val_df['time'].min()` proves the splits never overlap in time.
2. **Why Save as `.parquet`?**
   - Unlike CSV, **Parquet** preserves exact column data types (`datetime64`, `int32`, `float64`), is 3× smaller on disk, and lets `pd.read_parquet(..., columns=feature_cols)` load *only* the requested columns without reading the entire table into memory.
3. **Why Standardize with `(X - mu) / sd` Using Only `X_train`?**
   - Temperature is around `290` K, while solar generation reaches `5,000` MW. Machine learning models train much better when all features are centered around `0` with standard deviation `1`.
   - We compute the mean `mu = X_train_raw.mean(axis=0)` and standard deviation `sd = X_train_raw.std(axis=0)` **only on `X_train`**, then apply `(X - mu) / sd` to `X_train`, `X_val`, and `X_test`. Using `X_train`'s statistics on `Val` and `Test` prevents future test information from leaking into training.
4. **Why `np.ascontiguousarray(..., dtype=np.float32)`?**
   - Pandas defaults to 64-bit floats (`float64`, 8 bytes per number). Deep learning frameworks use 32-bit floats (`float32`, 4 bytes per number). Converting to contiguous `float32` cuts memory usage in half.

## Part 2: How to Read the 4 Diagrams in `eda.ipynb` (`plots/eda_4panel.png`)

Open [eda_4panel.png](file:///d:/HCMUT/HK261/P4AIDS/plots/eda_4panel.png) or view the output of **Cell 6** in [eda.ipynb](file:///d:/HCMUT/HK261/P4AIDS/eda.ipynb):

### Diagram 1 (Top-Left): `1. Distribution: Total Load Actual (MW)` — Histogram + KDE
- **What is on the axes?**
  - **X-axis (`total load actual`):** How much electricity (in Megawatts, MW) Spain consumed in an hour, from `~18,000 MW` to `~41,000 MW`.
  - **Y-axis (`Count`):** How many hours out of `35,064` fell into each bar.
  - **Smooth blue curve (`kde=True`):** The smooth density curve of the distribution.
- **How to read it & what it tells you:**
  - Notice the curve has **two peaks (a bimodal distribution)**:
    1. A lower peak around **`24,000–25,000 MW`** (nighttime and weekend hours when factories and offices are closed).
    2. A higher peak around **`31,000–32,000 MW`** (daytime working hours when industry and heating/cooling are active).
  - **Takeaway:** Because electricity demand splits into two distinct regimes (night/weekend vs. daytime work hours), **`hour` and `dayofweek` are essential features** for predicting electricity demand.

---

### Diagram 2 (Top-Right): `2. Relationship: Mean Temp (K) vs Total Load` — Scatter Plot (`hue='hour'`)
- **What is on the axes?**
  - **X-axis (`temp_mean`):** Average temperature across Spain in Kelvin (`273 K = 0°C`, `293 K = 20°C`, `305 K = 32°C`).
  - **Y-axis (`total load actual`):** Electricity demand in MW.
  - **Dot Color (`hue='hour'`):** Dark purple dots = early morning (`00:00–04:00`), blue/teal = morning (`08:00–12:00`), green = afternoon (`16:00`), bright yellow = evening (`20:00`).
- **How to read it & what it tells you:**
  - **Color layers (vertical separation):** Almost all **dark purple dots (`hour = 4`)** sit at the bottom (`20,000–26,000 MW`), while **teal, green, and yellow dots (`hours 12–20`)** sit near the top (`30,000–39,000 MW`). This shows that time of day sets the base level of electricity consumption.
  - **U-shaped temperature curve:** Looking at the daytime dots (green/teal), electricity load is high on the left when it is cold (`< 280 K / 7°C`, winter heating), lowest at mild temperatures (`288–292 K / 15–19°C`), and rises again on the right when it is hot (`> 300 K / 27°C`, summer air conditioning).
  - **Takeaway:** The relationship between temperature and electricity load is **non-linear (U-shaped)** and depends strongly on the hour of the day.

---

### Diagram 3 (Bottom-Left): `3. Category Comparison: Price Actual by Day of Week (0=Mon)` — Boxplot
- **What is on the axes?**
  - **X-axis (`dayofweek`):** Day of the week where `0 = Monday`, `1 = Tuesday`, `2 = Wednesday`, `3 = Thursday`, `4 = Friday`, `5 = Saturday`, `6 = Sunday`.
  - **Y-axis (`price actual`):** Electricity price in `€/MWh`.
- **How to read a Boxplot:**
  - **Horizontal line inside each colored box:** The **median** (50th percentile) price for that day.
  - **Bottom and top edges of the box:** The **25th percentile (`Q1`)** and **75th percentile (`Q3`)**—the middle 50% of prices fall inside the box.
  - **Whiskers (vertical lines with T-bars):** The normal range of prices (`1.5 × IQR`).
  - **Small circles beyond the whiskers:** Extreme price spikes or drops (outliers).
- **What it tells you:**
  - From **Monday (`0`) to Friday (`4`)**, the boxes sit at almost the exact same level (median around **`60 €/MWh`**).
  - On **Saturday (`5`)** and especially **Sunday (`6`)**, the entire box drops noticeably lower (Sunday median is around **`52 €/MWh`**).
  - **Takeaway:** Electricity prices drop significantly on weekends because industrial demand is lower.

---

### Diagram 4 (Bottom-Right): `4. Correlation Matrix (Spot Forecast vs Actual!)` — Heatmap
- **How to read a Correlation Heatmap:**
  - Each square shows the **Pearson correlation coefficient ($r$)** between two columns, from **`-1.00` (dark blue)** to **`+1.00` (dark red)**, with **`0.00` (grey/neutral)** in the middle:
    - **Near `+1.00` (Dark Red):** Both variables increase together in lockstep.
    - **Near `-1.00` (Dark Blue):** When one variable increases, the other decreases.
    - **Near `0.00` (Light/Grey):** Little or no linear relationship.
- **4 Key Insights from the Heatmap:**
  1. **`total load actual` vs `total load forecast` (`0.99` dark red square at top-left):**
     - These two columns have a **`0.99` correlation** because `total load forecast` is the official day-ahead forecast made by the Spanish grid operator.
     - **Important Modeling Note:** Do not use `total load forecast` as an input feature when training your own weather-based load forecasting model—otherwise your model will simply copy that column (`r = 0.99`) instead of learning from the weather! Instead, use `total load forecast` as a **benchmark** to compare your model's accuracy against.
  2. **`total load actual` vs `generation fossil gas` (`+0.55` orange):**
     - Fossil gas has the strongest positive correlation with national electricity load (`+0.55`) because gas power plants are ramped up quickly whenever national electricity demand peaks.
  3. **`generation solar` vs `temp_mean` (`+0.42`) and `humidity_mean` (`-0.58` blue):**
     - Solar power is positively correlated with temperature (`+0.42`) and negatively correlated with humidity (`-0.58`) because sunny daytime hours are warm and dry, while night and rainy/humid hours produce little solar power.
  4. **`temp_mean` vs `humidity_mean` (`-0.67` dark blue):**
     - In Spain's climate, hotter hours are much drier (`r = -0.67`).
